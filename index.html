Optical metasurfaces for the simulation of open-system quantum dynamics

View Paper ↗

Unitary evolution in quantum mechanics is a mathematical condition for the time evolution of a system, which encodes an obvious fact about the world: a physical system needs to be found in SOME state. This condition ensures that if we measure the system at any time, the probabilties of each possible outcome add up to one. However, it turns out that the effects of this condition are very different when our system has more than one degree of freedom. First, consider a single degree of freedom, say the polarization of a photon, which can be in one of two states: horizontal polarization \(\ket{H}\) and vertical polarization \(\ket{V}\). Suppose, at a time \(t=0\), the photon's state, which we label \(\ket{\psi}\) is in the horizontally polarized state: \(ket{\psi} = \ket{H}\). Then we allow the photon to evolve in time, potentially changing it's state, so that at a time \(t\), the probability that the photon is in the state \(\ket{H}\) is given by a function \(P_H(t)\). Then, the principle of unitary evolution tells us that the probability that the photon is in the state \(\ket{V}\) is \(1 - P_H(t)\). It encodes the simple fact that if the photon is not horizontally polarized, then it must be vertically polarized. Simple enough.
